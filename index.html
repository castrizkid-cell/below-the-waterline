01  ·  Deep time

The last of its line

People call crocodiles living fossils. That gets it backwards. Over 250 million years, the crocodile's lineage also produced two-legged runners with beaks, armoured plant eaters, and sea hunters with flippers and a tail fin. All of them are gone. The shape in the river is the one that kept working.

I≈252–247 Ma

Two branches

The story starts after the worst extinction the planet has had. About 252 million years ago, at the end of the Permian, volcanic eruptions in what is now Siberia went on for hundreds of thousands of years. They heated the climate and took the oxygen out of the oceans. Most species on Earth died.

In the emptied world that followed, a group of reptiles called the archosaurs, the "ruling reptiles", spread quickly and split in two. One branch would lead to pterosaurs, dinosaurs and, in the end, birds. The other, the pseudosuchians, leads to the crocodile.

This is the most useful single fact about the animal: a crocodile is a bird's cousin, not a lizard's. You can see it in the body. Crocodiles and birds both have a four-chambered heart. In 2010, researchers showed that air flows one way through parts of an alligator's lungs, a system once thought to belong only to birds. Both groups build nests and guard them. In both, the young call from inside the egg before they hatch. A Nile crocodile listening for chirps under the sand is doing something a hen would recognise.

II247–201 Ma

When the croc line ruled

For the first fifty million years the dinosaurs were not in charge. The croc line was. The largest predators on Triassic floodplains were rauisuchians such as Postosuchus and Prestosuchus: deep-skulled, serrated-toothed, up to seven metres long, standing with their legs straight under the body. Below them grazed aetosaurs, low armoured plant eaters with spikes along their shoulders.

The croc line also made animals that make no sense as crocodiles. Effigia, from New Mexico, walked on its hind legs, had no teeth, and had a beak. It looked so much like the later ostrich-shaped dinosaurs that its bones sat unrecognised in a museum block from the 1940s until 2006.

In the rivers lived phytosaurs: long snouts, armoured backs, ambush from the shallows. They looked almost exactly like crocodiles, but most recent studies place them outside the crocodile line altogether. Their nostrils sat on a mound near the eyes rather than at the tip of the snout. Evolution had already built a crocodile once, from different parts. It would do it again.

III201 Ma

The first filter

About 201 million years ago the supercontinent Pangaea began to tear open along the line that became the Atlantic. Lava flooded an area larger than Europe and poured carbon dioxide into the air. In the extinction that followed, the rauisuchians, the aetosaurs, the beaked poposauroids and the phytosaurs all disappeared.

From the whole croc line, one group came through: the crocodylomorphs. They looked nothing like the animal in the Nile. Terrestrisuchus, found in Triassic cave fills in South Wales, was a slim runner about a metre long, most of it tail, with long legs held under the body. It probably ran on its toes.

The animals that carried the crocodile line through this extinction were small enough to carry under one arm. With their large competitors gone, the dinosaurs took over the land. The crocodile line went into every gap the dinosaurs left.

The modern crocodile is not the original design. It is the one that was left.

IV200–66 Ma

Everything else they tried

Over the next 135 million years, crocodile relatives lived in places and ways no crocodile does now.

At sea. The thalattosuchians left the shore. The metriorhynchids gave up their back armour, turned their legs into flippers, and grew a tail fin like a shark's, with the end of the spine bent down into the lower lobe. Large salt glands in the head handled the seawater. They probably never came back to land.

On land, eating plants. On the southern continents the notosuchians went back to dry ground and became almost anything. Simosuchus, from Madagascar, had a short blunt face and leaf-shaped teeth for chewing vegetation. Pakasuchus, from Tanzania about 105 million years ago, was the size of a cat, with cheek teeth that sheared past each other like a mammal's. Kaprosuchus, from Niger, had three pairs of tusks that stuck up and down out of its closed jaws.

As giants. Sarcosuchus hunted rivers in what is now the Sahara of Niger about 112 million years ago. It was around nine metres long, twice a big Nile crocodile. In North America, Deinosuchus, an early relative of alligators, reached about ten metres and left its tooth marks in dinosaur bones.

At the water's edge. One group, the neosuchians, settled into the margins of rivers and lakes: flat heads, eyes and nostrils on top, a long swimming tail. Around 125 million years ago a branch of them, the eusuchians, finished a change to the skull that matters more than any other.

By about 90 million years ago the first true crocodilians had appeared, and they soon split into the three branches still alive today: alligators and caimans, gharials, and crocodiles.

V66 Ma

The second filter

When the asteroid hit at Chicxulub, it ended the non-bird dinosaurs, the pterosaurs and the great marine reptiles. Crocodile relatives were hit hard too, but crocodilians, the sea-going dyrosaurids and the land-hunting sebecids came through.

The likeliest reasons are the ones you can still see in a Nile crocodile. It is cold-blooded and can go months between meals. It lives in fresh water, where food chains run partly on dead and rotting plant matter, so they held up better than food chains on land that needed living plants and sunlight. And it can wait: dug into a bank, or still in the shallows, for as long as it takes.

There may be a twist. Features of the crocodile's heart and lungs suggest to some physiologists that its Triassic ancestors were active, warm-blooded animals, closer to birds in how they lived. If that hypothesis is right, the slow crocodile is not primitive. Its ancestors gave up a high-energy life, and the low-energy one they settled into is part of why they survived.

VI66 Ma – today

Slow, not stuck

After the asteroid, crocodilians spread across a warm world. Their variety rises and falls with global temperature. In the hot Eocene, around 50 million years ago, alligator relatives lived on Ellesmere Island, inside the Arctic Circle. As the planet cooled, their range pulled back toward the tropics, and the number of species fell.

Their genes changed slowly too. When the genomes of an alligator, a saltwater crocodile and a gharial were compared in 2014, they had built up changes more slowly than those of birds or mammals. Studies of body size and shape find the same thing: once crocodilians reached a design that worked at the water's edge, it stayed.

"Living fossil" suggests something left behind. Survivor is closer. Fewer than thirty species are alive now, and every one is built to the same plan: eyes and nostrils on top, a long tail, an airway at the back of the throat, and patience.

Africa didn't just receive the Nile crocodile. It made it, then sent its relatives across an ocean.

VII≈10 Ma – today

Then, Africa

The true crocodiles, the genus Crocodylus, appeared roughly ten million years ago, give or take a few million. Where they appeared is still argued. Early DNA studies pointed to Asia. Fossils have since pulled the answer toward Africa: Crocodylus checchiai, from rocks about seven million years old at As Sahabi in Libya, turns out to be closest to the crocodiles of the Americas.

East Africa's Rift Valley lakes were crowded with crocodiles. Around Lake Turkana several species lived side by side, among them the needle-snouted Euthecodon and Crocodylus thorbjarnarsoni, which may have reached seven metres. At Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania, about 1.8 million years ago, a horned crocodile named Crocodylus anthropophagus, "man-eater", hunted the same lake as early humans. Hominin bones from the gorge carry crocodile tooth marks. Our ancestors drank at its water's edge too.

The Nile crocodile came out of this crowd. DNA puts its closest living relatives across the ocean, in the Americas. Somewhere around five to seven million years ago, crocodiles from Africa crossed the Atlantic, probably drifting on currents. The salt glands on the tongue that still let Nile crocodiles live in estuaries made the trip possible. Their descendants are the American, Orinoco, Morelet's and Cuban crocodiles.

A second relative turned out to be hiding in plain sight. In 2011, DNA from living animals and from Egyptian temple mummies showed that the crocodiles of West and Central Africa are a separate species, Crocodylus suchus. Some of the mummies were suchus. The Egyptians may have known what biologists had missed: there were two kinds of crocodile in the Nile, and one was easier to live with.

02  ·  Field notes

How it hunts

The lunge at the water's edge is the famous part, and the rarest. A Nile crocodile eats mostly fish, and it has a different method for nearly everything that comes near water. What ties them together is patience, a skin that feels the water, and a bite that does not let go.

The bite

Built to shut

16,414 NThe strongest bite ever measured in a living animal: a 4.59 m saltwater crocodile. Roughly the weight of a small car pressing down through the teeth.
Size, not shapeIn tests across all 23 crocodilian species, bite force tracked body size. A Nile crocodile bites about as hard as a saltwater crocodile its size.
Weak to openThe opening muscles are small. The power is all in closing, which is why a crocodile's jaws can be held shut with a strap.

The teeth

Cones that grip

Every tooth is a hollow cone with its replacement already growing inside it, and a third starting beneath. When a tooth breaks on bone, the next is ready. A Nile crocodile has 64 to 68 teeth and can replace each of them dozens of times. They are built to grip and puncture, not to slice, which is why everything after the bite depends on drowning, twisting and swallowing whole.

The death roll

How a spin cuts

In 2007 researchers filmed alligators rolling and found the spin isn't powered by the legs. It comes from the body bending, the head and tail swung to one side, which a tucked, streamlined animal turns into rotation. A 2019 survey found death rolls in most crocodilian species tested, including slender-snouted ones that mostly eat fish.

A year at the crossing

The feast has a season

Herds cross the MaraRains

For most of the year, the crocodiles of the Mara live on fish and whatever comes to drink. Then, as the rains in the south end, more than a million wildebeest move north into Kenya, and the river is in their way.

They cross at the same places, year after year, often several times as the grazing shifts. Big crocodiles may eat a few large meals in those months and then very little until the next season. A cold-blooded body makes that possible: a Nile crocodile needs only a fraction of the food a lion of the same weight would.

The timing moves with the rains. Some years the herds reach the river in June, some years not until August.

After the meal

What happens next

  1. SecondsThe strike. Jaws close, the animal is pulled off balance.
  2. MinutesDrowning. Large prey is held under until it stops moving.
  3. An hourTearing and swallowing. Pieces go down whole, head held high.
  4. Hours to daysIt hauls out to bask. A warmer body digests faster, so it chases the sun.
  5. DaysVery strong stomach acid dissolves bone. Hair, horn and hooves resist it.
  6. Weeks to monthsIt may not need another large meal. It goes back to waiting.

A hunter that never hurries. It doesn't need to be faster than you. It only needs you to drink.

I

Big water, big bodies

The four giants (saltwater, Nile, Orinoco and American crocodiles) live in large rivers, estuaries and along coasts, where big prey comes to the water and a big body has room to hunt. The smallest species live in forest swamps, small lakes and on islands: the Philippine crocodile, the Cuban crocodile, Morelet's in the Yucatán.

II

Twice as long, eight times as heavy

Length is a poor guide to weight. Weight rises roughly with the cube of length, so a 6 m crocodile weighs about eight times as much as a 3 m one. A 3 m crocodile weighs about as much as two people. Lolong, 6.17 m, weighed 1,075 kg.

III

The head is the diet

A narrow snout cuts through water fast and sideways, and suits fish. The Australian freshwater crocodile is the extreme. A broad snout resists the twisting of large, struggling prey: the saltwater crocodile takes pigs, deer and even cattle, and the mugger takes deer as well as fish. Most species sit in between and eat both.

IV

Records are slippery

Males of every species grow much larger than females and keep growing slowly for decades, so a few old males set every record. Many famous lengths came from tanned skins, which stretch, or from guesses at animals in the water. The numbers on this sheet are cautious ones.

How a count is made

Count the eyes

Most crocodile numbers start in a boat at night. A spotlight sweeps the bank, and crocodile eyes shine back red-orange, because a reflective layer behind the retina bounces the light back out.

Experienced counters judge each animal's size from the gap between its eyes and nostrils. But anything underwater, behind reeds or wary of the light is missed. A count is a minimum, and survey teams correct for what they could not see.

How they came back

The ranching loop

In some programmes a share of farm-raised young has been released back into the wild. Ranching works best where it is tied to surveys and quotas; where it is not, it can hide illegal trade.

E

What still threatens them

Five pressures

Conflict with people

Nile crocodiles kill more people than any other crocodile. Many attacks go unrecorded, so reliable totals don't exist. Revenge killings and the removal of "problem" animals follow.

Lost riverbanks

Dams, irrigation, sand mining and farming on riverbanks take the sandbars where females nest and the quiet backwaters where young crocodiles hide.

Polluted water

Upstream mining, farming and sewage have been linked to die-offs, as in the Olifants River. As top predators, crocodiles concentrate whatever is in the fish they eat.

Nets and hooks

Crocodiles drown in gill nets and take fish from them. That puts them in direct competition with the fishing communities that share their water.

Warmer nests

Nest temperature decides whether an egg becomes male or female. Warming could shift those ratios. Females can respond by nesting in shadier or deeper spots, and how much this will matter is not yet known.

08  ·  Stories, checked

Lore

Myth, probably

The toothpick bird

Herodotus wrote that a bird walks into the crocodile's open mouth to eat leeches. The Egyptian plover is often named. Nobody has reliably photographed or filmed it happening.

True

They fish as a team

Crocodiles have been seen lining up across a channel or forming a semicircle to herd fish, each taking what swims past. At a large carcass, one anchors it while others roll.

True

Cave crocodiles

In Ankarana, in northern Madagascar, crocodiles retreat into flooded caves during the dry season, sometimes far from daylight.

True

Saved by bad skin

Lake Turkana's crocodiles have skin so full of bone plates that it was nearly worthless to hide hunters. In the 1960s a survey counted roughly 12,000 there, one of the densest populations ever recorded.

Legend

Gustave

A huge male in Burundi's Rusizi River and on the shores of Lake Tanganyika, often estimated at around 6 m. The many killings blamed on him have never been verified, and he hasn't been reliably seen in years.

True

Salt tolerance

Salt glands on the tongue let Nile crocodiles live in estuaries like St Lucia in South Africa, and sometimes along the coast.

Next time you see two bumps on still water, remember what's underneath.
